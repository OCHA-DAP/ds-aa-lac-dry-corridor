10  2026 Primera Situation & Priorities

10.1 Purpose

ROLAC missions to El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala (July 2026) are supporting country-level analysis of El Niño humanitarian impacts. This chapter is CHD’s contribution to the preliminary desk review, and it follows one line of reasoning: a drought is already underway, it is unlikely to relent this season, and because it is region-wide the useful question is not whether but where to concentrate attention. Each section builds toward that answer — first the current situation, then the forecast, then the sub-national targeting.

NoteBottom line
  1. A dry start, confirmed and region-wide. Two independent rainfall products put the May–June primera at roughly two-thirds of normal in every admin-1 unit of all three countries.
  2. The season is unlikely to recover. The forecast keeps the rest of primera dry, and points to a dry postrera as well — so the usual “second harvest” fallback looks compromised. (That postrera signal is low-confidence: SEAS5’s postrera skill is weak, so it is a flag to watch, not a firm prediction — see Chapter 16.)
  3. So the question is where to act — and in which season. A region-wide deficit does not localize priorities; that comes from where the drought meets active, rain-sensitive cropland (JRC/FAO ASAP). Split by season: primera crop stress is already realized in Olancho, El Paraíso, Choluteca (Honduras), La Unión (El Salvador), and Zacapa (Guatemala) — act now; a dry postrera is forecast (low confidence) for Guatemala’s Pacific belt and central El Salvador — watch; San Vicente and Morazán face both; and the western highlands sit off this seasonal calendar — a vulnerability question, not a seasonal-drought one.

11 The current situation

Observed rainfall, May–June (primera to date). Every admin-1 unit is in the driest third of its own 1981–2025 record. The two independent products with June data — ERA5 (reanalysis) and IMERG (satellite) — both put the May–June deficit well below normal in all three countries (median 65–81% of normal). They agree on the regional signal but differ on severity (ERA5 is consistently the most severe), so we report the regional deficit with confidence but avoid over-precise per-unit “record” claims. CHIRPS (the FEWS NET standard) lags ~5 weeks, so it covers May only; it too shows May below normal.

The map below shows the empirical return period of the observed May–June deficit: much of the region is experiencing a 1-in-10-year-or-rarer dry start, and parts of Honduras and eastern Guatemala a 1-in-20-year-or-rarer one.

12 The season is unlikely to recover

The map above is only the primera so far. To see where the season is likely to end up, we extend it: combining the observed May–June rainfall (ERA5) with the SEAS5 July-issuance forecast for July–August gives a complete estimate of the full primera — our best single view of the 2026 season. It is the same story, and if anything darker: the full season is dry almost everywhere, at a 1-in-10 to 1-in-20-year-or-rarer level across much of the region. This is the map to anchor on.

Why so dry? Because both halves of the season point the same way. Decomposing the estimate into its observed start (May–June) and its forecast remainder (July–August), nearly every unit is below normal in both — the forecast does not rescue the units that started dry (top-right = driest in both). Only Izabal (Guatemala) has a near-normal forecast despite a dry start.

No second harvest to fall back on. In this bimodal system a failed primera is often assumed to leave the postrera (Sep–Nov, the main bean season) as a second chance. Not this year: the SEAS5 forecast puts the postrera dry too. Plotting the complete primera against the postrera forecast, every admin-1 unit falls in the top-right — dry in both seasons — so the developing El Niño removes the fallback across the board. (This is the double-season failure the drought literature associates with El Niño years such as 2014–2015.)

The same view in return-period terms (the “1-in-N years” framing) tells the same story in more citable units — most of the region faces a 1-in-20-year-or-rarer season in both windows. Note the primera axis saturates: the complete primera is the driest on record (≈1-in-46+ years) for most units, so those points pile against the record ceiling and are jittered slightly to be visible. The σ version above discriminates better among those record-dry units.

One caveat governs how much weight to put on the postrera half. Validating SEAS5 against 45 years of observed drought (ROC-AUC), primera skill is reasonable but postrera skill is weak (median AUC 0.65; acceptable in only 11 of 54 admin-1 units — see Chapter 16). So the complete primera picture is firm (mostly observed), but the postrera “no relief” signal is a low-confidence outlook to watch, not a prediction — a point carried through the targeting below.

13 Targeting: beyond the regional hazard

The rainfall picture is unambiguous but uniform — dry everywhere, both seasons — so it does not tell the mission where to concentrate. Two further layers do: where the drought is already translating into agricultural stress, and which areas are structurally most sensitive to El Niño and so most likely to deteriorate.

13.1 Agricultural impact (FAO ASI)

FAO’s Agricultural Stress Index (share of crop area in severe stress, cumulative to late June) is well above normal in Honduras (11% vs 7%), near normal in El Salvador, and below normal in Guatemala — so despite dry rainfall, Guatemala’s crops are not (yet) unusually stressed, while the impact is concentrating in Honduras and eastern El Salvador. Read as a return period — how unusual each unit’s current stress is against its own 1984–2025 history (Weibull, the same basis as the rainfall maps) — the leading units are rare events: Olancho and San Vicente near 1-in-15-year stress, Morazán ~1-in-11, Choluteca ~1-in-9, and El Paraíso and La Unión ~1-in-7. The table in Section 13.4 carries this return period per unit.

Current stress, though, is a lagging signal — it marks where damage has already begun. To anticipate where an intensifying El Niño will bite hardest over the rest of the season, we add a second, structural lens.

13.2 El Niño structural sensitivity

A rapidly developing El Niño (the ONI anomaly rose from −0.4 in January to +1.5 by June 2026) frames the season. El Niño depresses both seasons here, but the primera signal is the stronger of the two (a mean primera deficit of up to ~−31% in past El Niño years versus ~−18% for the postrera), so we show primera as the structural indicator; the postrera map is similar in pattern but weaker (in Chapter 17). The map shows each unit’s mean primera rainfall anomaly in past El Niño years (1981–2025); the eastern/southern dry corridor dries out most, and 83% of admin-1 units show a statistically significant El Niño drying signal. Reassuringly, these historical “El Niño losers” are largely the same eastern-Honduras and eastern-El-Salvador units where stress is already building today — current impact and structural risk point to the same places.

13.3 Is this cropland, and is it the right season?

Two checks turn the hazard into agricultural risk. Is it cropland? The JRC/FAO ASAP crop mask shows the dry corridor is heavily agricultural — 42–65% cropland by country. (The MODIS layer we first tried badly underestimated smallholder farming — it put Olancho, the most-stressed unit, at ~2% — so ASAP is the exposure layer to trust.) Is our drought hitting the rain-critical part of the season? ASAP’s precipitation-sensitive-period (PSP) layer marks May–November as the rain-critical window across essentially all of that cropland — except the western highlands (Honduras: Lempira, Intibucá, Copán, Ocotepeque, La Paz; Guatemala: Huehuetenango) and the Caribbean fringe (Izabal, Islas de la Bahía), whose crops run on a different calendar. For those off-calendar zones a primera/postrera deficit is the wrong lens — they belong to the broader food-security / vulnerability question, not the seasonal-drought one.

13.4 Season-specific agricultural drought risk

For the crop-relevant, in-season zones, the risk splits cleanly by season. Primera risk is realized — read from the crop stress already on the ground (FAO ASI). Postrera risk is forecast — read from the SEAS5 September–November outlook, and therefore low-confidence (weak forecast skill, Chapter 16). This is the desk-review headline:

  • Primera — biting now: Olancho, El Paraíso, Choluteca (Honduras); La Unión (El Salvador); Zacapa (Guatemala). Crops materially stressed today.
  • Postrera — forecast, watch: Guatemala’s Pacific belt (Escuintla, Suchitepéquez, San Marcos, Quetzaltenango, Retalhuleu, Santa Rosa) + central El Salvador (San Miguel, Usulután, Cabañas, Cuscatlán) + Honduras’s Valle. Driest in the SON forecast, not yet stressed.
  • Both: San Vicente and Morazán (eastern El Salvador) — already stressed and driest postrera forecast.
  • Off-calendar: western highlands + Caribbean — assessed through vulnerability data, not this.

In short. The 2026 primera is a real, region-wide drought unlikely to relent this season. Where it meets active, rain-sensitive cropland it becomes agricultural drought risk — and that splits cleanly: primera crop stress is already realized in southern/eastern Honduras, eastern El Salvador, and Zacapa (act now); a dry postrera is forecast for Guatemala’s Pacific belt and central El Salvador (watch, low confidence); and the western highlands sit off this seasonal calendar, better assessed through vulnerability / food-security data. That is the desk-review basis for the in-country discussions.

14 Caveats & data sources

  • Product disagreement on severity. ERA5, IMERG, and CHIRPS agree the region is dry but differ on magnitude (ERA5 most severe; recent ERA5 months are provisional). Treat the regional signal as robust and per-unit “driest on record” claims as product-dependent.
  • Crop exposure & season = JRC/FAO ASAP. Cropland fraction, growing-season timing, and the precipitation-sensitive period all come from ASAP (crop mask + in-season + PSP layers). An earlier MODIS 500 m cropland layer badly underestimated smallholder agriculture here (Olancho ~2%) and is not used. ASAP’s PSP treats the bimodal season as one continuous May–November rain-critical window, so it gates whether a zone is in-season but does not itself split primera vs postrera — that split comes from the season-specific hazard (ASI vs SEAS5 SON).
  • Season-specific confidence differs. The primera risk is realized (observed rainfall + current ASI stress) — high confidence. The postrera risk is forecast (SEAS5 SON) with weak skill — treat as a watch signal, not a prediction.
  • CHIRPS latency. CHIRPS final lags ~5 weeks, so it corroborates May only; ERA5 (to June) and IMERG (to July) carry the more current read.
  • ASI is cumulative. We use the final June dekad (season-to-date), compared to the same dekad historically — not a multi-dekad mean.
  • Forecast resolution. SEAS5’s ~36 km pixels are coarse relative to small admin units, especially in El Salvador (mostly sub-pixel at admin-1). The complete-primera map is shown at admin-1 for all three countries because its observed half (ERA5 May–June) resolves admin-1 fine; within-El-Salvador forecast detail (the July–August half) should be read as indicative, not precise. Observational products (CHIRPS 5 km, ASI) genuinely resolve admin-1/2.

14.0.1 Data provenance (pull scripts — shown, not executed here)

The heavy pulls run against the team database and Google Earth Engine outside this document; their outputs are the committed CSVs read above. Representative CHIRPS pull (rgee):

Code 
# artefacts/mission_support_2026/_shared/pull_chirps_gee.R (abridged)
chirps <- ee$ImageCollection("UCSB-CHG/CHIRPS/DAILY")
fc <- ee$FeatureCollection("FAO/GAUL_SIMPLIFIED_500m/2015/level1")$
  filter(ee$Filter$inList("ADM0_NAME", ee$List(list("Guatemala","Honduras","El Salvador"))))
# monthly sum per year -> zonal mean over GAUL admin units (via getInfo, no Drive scope needed)
ee_extract(x = year_image(yr, months), y = fc, fun = ee$Reducer$mean(), scale = 5566, via = "getInfo")

SEAS5 / ERA5 / IMERG are read from the team Postgres database (cumulus::pg_con()), FAO ASI via cumulus::fao_asi_adm1_tabular(), and ONI via cumulus::load_oni().

15 Appendix — impact & structure priority tiers (exploratory)

Before layering in the ASAP crop calendar, an earlier synthesis ranked units into Act / Watch / Monitor purely from current agricultural impact (ASI) and structural El Niño sensitivity. The season-specific view in Section 13.4 supersedes it (it adds crop exposure, the precipitation-sensitive-period gate, and the primera/postrera split), but the tiers are kept here for reference — the Act set is identical to the realized primera-risk units.

  • Act — ≥15% of crop area already in severe stress (FAO ASI).
  • Watch — not yet materially stressed, but historically ≥15% drier in past El Niño years.
  • Monitor — dry like everywhere else, no material impact yet, not highly sensitive.

16 Appendix — SEAS5 forecast skill

The “no relief” postrera signal rests on the SEAS5 September–November forecast, so its skill sets how much weight it can bear. Validating each admin-1 unit’s SEAS5 forecast against 45 years of observed drought (ROC-AUC, following the book’s forecast-skill method; AUC > 0.7 = acceptable, 0.5 = no better than chance), the two seasons differ sharply. Primera skill is reasonable (median AUC 0.73; acceptable in 36 of 54 units), consistent with the firm, mostly-observed primera picture. Postrera skill is weak (median AUC 0.65; acceptable in only 11 of 54 units) — barely better than chance across much of Guatemala. That is why the primera picture is treated as firm and the postrera as a low-confidence watch signal throughout.

17 Appendix — El Niño sensitivity, postrera
